Purpose, and the split is the point.

**CR-11's SAFE half.** Six spellings report where the row
`silence-operator-around-global-receiver` says an operator wrapping a global receiver is silent in
every spelling: the comma sequence, bare parentheses, an `as` assertion, a non-null assertion, and —
found by the verifier and not by the review — the `eval` and bare-`fetch` twins of the comma sequence.
**The error runs in the SAFE direction: the gate reaches further than its text.** Wave 28 disclosed one
instance in the ledger prose *unprompted*, and the mechanism it named there — `unwrap` strips the
wrapper before any receiver resolver is reached — is the correct and **complete** explanation for all
six. A residual that understates the gate cannot cause anyone to ship an outbound call believing it
would be caught. This half is an **overreach to correct**, and this plan corrects it without borrowing
blocker weight it does not carry.

**CR-11's UNSAFE half, which nobody named.** `const g = globalThis ?? self; g.fetch(url)` reports
nothing, and so do the conditional twin and the `fetch`, `eval` and `navigator` initializer forms.
`const g = globalThis ?? self` is a **plausible defensive idiom**, not a contrivance. It creates a
fully aliased global receiver the gate cannot see, no residual clause anywhere names it, and a reader
is **actively misled into believing it is covered**: the `initializerReceiver` row says initializer
position and call position give the same answer, and the `receiverKind` row demonstrates the SDK
operator working. Read together they say the global case transfers. It does not. That is what upgrades
CR-11 to blocker weight, and it is what this plan closes at the seam — one shared descent, reached from
the global resolvers the way the SDK resolvers already reach it, rather than five copies.

**The five warnings.** WR-33: two exemption reasons are wrong, and the verifier split them — `collect`'s
is **materially false** because its inline operator test decides which right-hand expressions ever reach
a resolver, which is the entirety of CR-12; `visit`'s is **misleading rather than false** because each
individual resolution IS delegated and what is unregistered is the DISPATCH. That distinction is
honoured rather than flattened. WR-34: the one case titled for CORE-11's box matches both states and
nothing anywhere pins it — the smallest finding in the report and the one sitting closest to the wound.
WR-35: all three head-side disclosures name one mechanism where the sweep finds two, and **the one
offset the fixture builds its exemplars on is the one they do not describe** — while the same docblock
states both correctly sixty lines above. WR-36: round 5's own IN-25 correction introduced a narrower
overstatement in the paragraph that says why that matters. WR-37: a nested destructure of a receiver is
silent while both halves report — a **composition of two shapes the gate already resolves**, which is a
different class from "a shape nobody thought of".

**CORE-11's discharge, and `[ ]` remains a correct outcome.** Wave 28 discharged 7 of 8 rows and named
one blocking row; the verifier executed the table and found **6 of 8 carry a named blocking shape**,
finding two itself. Waves 30 and 31 and this plan's widenings address those shapes, but this plan does
not presume the flip. It re-executes every row in this session, and the box moves only if every
enumerated surface discharges. Whatever the answer is, `CORE11_BOX_EXPECTED` is set to it and the suite
asserts it — because the box has been flipped early twice and reverted twice, and the one test named
for it could not see it.

**SEVERITY, STATED PLAINLY AND NOT INFLATED.** Nothing leaks and nothing leaked. No outbound call
exists in any non-spec source under either root, the gate runs green over the real tree inside a
1200-test suite, `check:bundle` reports the shipped bundle's entire import set as one specifier

finding here is PROSPECTIVE blindness in a test-only gate, and WR-35 and WR-36 are warnings on
